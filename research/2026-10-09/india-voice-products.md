# Indian business voice products: evidence and product choices

**Research date:** 2026-10-09
**Method:** static review of first-party public pages and documentation. No account, trial call, provider invocation, installation, or listening test was used. Vendor scale, latency, quality, and outcome figures below are vendor-published unless stated otherwise.

## What adoption is actually visible

The clearest public evidence of Indian business adoption in this set belongs to Sarvam. Its official customer index says Mahindra Finance has used voice agents for more than one crore calls across 12 languages. A separate EkStep/Sarvam impact report says a 31-day programme with 20 government and nonprofit organisations used 74 lakh voice minutes and reached about 50 lakh unique users; these are programme-reported figures, not an independent audit. Sarvam's Tata Capital/MoEngage case reports that 41% of called customers stayed to converse, nearly all conversations were in Indic languages, and about one in six conversations produced a qualified lead. These examples show real demand in finance, public services, agriculture, health and skilling, while remaining vendor case studies rather than general market-share evidence ([customer stories](https://www.sarvam.ai/stories), [Listen at Scale](https://www.sarvam.ai/stories/ekstep), [Tata Capital/MoEngage](https://www.sarvam.ai/stories/how-sarvam-and-moengage-make-voice-part-of-the-customer-journey)).

Smallest.ai publishes a mature platform and customer logos, but the reviewed official pages did not provide comparable named India deployment volumes. Dialflo's public webhook example is an Indian Hindi recruitment workflow, but an example payload is not adoption evidence. Alchemic presents Indian research use cases and example dashboards; no reviewed page names a customer or audited call volume. Treat Sarvam as the adoption leader in this comparison and the others as products to evaluate, not as proven equivalents.

## Implications for Relay and the Azure grant

No reviewed vendor documents a way to charge its hosted speech, agent or telephony usage to Azure credits. “OpenAI-compatible” describes an API shape, not Azure billing. Sarvam's BYOK pages describe Sarvam calling customer APIs, while its only documented private model deployment is AWS. Smallest lists OpenAI models inside Atoms but does not document a buyer-supplied Azure OpenAI endpoint. Dialflo and Alchemic publish no Azure integration evidence.

Therefore keep Azure as Relay's authorised paid reasoning path and treat every hosted voice vendor as a separate spend requiring explicit approval. A provider-neutral adapter can send transcript/context into Relay and retain Azure reasoning, but latency, data flow and tool ownership must be tested. Free credits can support a bounded comparison without implying grant compatibility.

## Three concrete product decisions

1. **Run a Sarvam speech-layer and Voice Agents evaluation first.** It has the strongest India deployment evidence, explicit code-mix/key-term support, phone transfer, versioned tests and India residency. Test Hindi-English plus one South Indian language using real names, amounts, abbreviations, interruptions, noise and the actual telephony provider; do not infer those results from vendor scale.
2. **Use Smallest as the controlled challenger.** Its pronunciation dictionary, turn/interruption controls, warm transfer, versioning, per-call trace/cost detail and public unit pricing make a comparison diagnosable. Pin identical scenarios and human listening rubrics; reject prompt score as a release gate by itself.
3. **Do not integrate Dialflo or Alchemic into Relay's runtime yet.** Ask Dialflo for a recorded, scenario-based demo covering code-switching, barge-in, transfer, retries, price and API/version lifecycle. Use Alchemic only for a tightly scoped founder/customer research pilot when its managed recruiting and evidence drill-down justify study pricing.

The release gate should be Relay-owned: scripted text checks, repeated simulated conversations, recorded audio review by native speakers, real phone-path tests, tool/transfer receipts, cost per successful outcome, and regression cases created from production failures.
